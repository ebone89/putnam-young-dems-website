Putnam County Voter Guide
-------------------------

The Putnam County Voter Resource Guide: A Policy and Electoral Analysis of the 2026 Primaries and General Election
==================================================================================================================

The upcoming 2026 electoral cycle in Putnam County, Florida, represents a critical juncture for local governance, fiscal structure, and community development. Set against a backdrop of unique demographic challenges, changing state legislative boundaries, and sweeping constitutional proposals, the choices made by voters will resonate for years. This report provides a comprehensive, objective, and deeply analytical resource to guide Putnam County residents through the primary election on August 18, 2026, and the general election on November 3, 2026.   
Partisan Realities and Voting Logistics
---------------------------------------

The operational mechanics of Florida’s electoral system require careful planning by voters, particularly given the state's closed primary rules. Under Florida law, only voters registered with a specific political party may cast ballots for that party’s candidates in primary contests. However, all registered voters, including those registered with No Party Affiliation, are eligible to vote in nonpartisan contests—such as judicial and school board races—and on local referendums and constitutional amendments during the primary.

Federal and State Legislative Profiles
--------------------------------------

Federal and state legislative contests on the 2026 ballot will shape representation for Putnam County residents in Washington, D.C., and Tallahassee. The seats feature high-stakes primary battles and strategic general election dynamics.   

### U.S. Senate (Special Election)

Following Marco Rubio’s resignation to serve as U.S. Secretary of State, Governor Ron DeSantis appointed Ashley Moody to fill the vacant Senate seat. Moody is now running in this special election to secure voter approval to serve the remainder of the term. The Republican primary features incumbent Senator Moody against three challengers: Chris Gleason, an Army National Guard veteran and data forensics consultant; Neelam Perry, a physician whose medical licenses in Florida and New York were revoked following state disciplinary actions; and Ernest "Ernie" Rivera, a minister, educator, and entrepreneur who previously challenged Rubio in the 2016 primary.   

The Democratic primary is a contest between Angie Nixon and Alex Vindman. Nixon, a state representative from Jacksonville, community organizer, and political scientist, is running on a progressive platform. Vindman, a retired U.S. Army lieutenant colonel, former National Security Council director, and prominent figure in federal congressional inquiries, has leveraged a nationwide fundraising network, accumulating over $8.1 million.

### U.S. House of Representatives: District 6

Putnam County lies within Florida's 6th Congressional District. The Republican primary is a competitive contest between incumbent Representative Randy Fine and social media influencer Dan Bilzerian. Fine, an entrepreneur first elected to the Florida House in 2016 before winning a congressional special election in April 2025, has deep ties to traditional Republican fundraising networks. Fine’s campaign has raised over $2.2 million, supported by organizations like the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), and he has focused his platform on economic development, school choice, and strong national security measures.   

Bilzerian, a U.S. Navy veteran and the founder of Ignite International Brands, entered the race with a self-funded campaign, loaning his committee $1 million. His campaign has focused on non-interventionist foreign policy, criticizing federal spending and Fine's rhetoric. The primary also features Palm Coast City Council member Charles Gambaro, who has raised $338,000, and Aaron Baker, who has raised $106,000.   

The Democratic primary features Robert David Cooper II, Steve Morgan, Ronnie "Ron" Murchinson-Rivera, and Eric Yonce. While the redrawn congressional boundaries favor the Republican nominee in the November general election, the district's final composition makes turnout in agricultural communities like Putnam County a key factor.   

### Florida State Senate: District 8

The District 8 State Senate seat, being vacated by the outgoing and term-limited Tommy Wright, represents a redrawn legislative boundary with a distinct Republican voting history (R+22.0 based on recent presidential returns). Because only one candidate from each major party qualified for the ballot, the primaries were canceled.   

Republican Representative Jake Johansson, carrying endorsements from Senate President Ben Albritton and major business organizations like the Florida Chamber of Commerce, automatically advanced to the general election. He will face Democratic nominee Judy Ngying and Libertarian candidate Gabriel Jude Wozniak in November.   

### Florida House of Representatives: District 20

District 20 is the primary state legislative seat representing Putnam County, covering the entire county along with parts of Clay, Marion, and St. Johns counties. Incumbent Republican Representative Judson Sapp, a local business executive and CEO of W.J. Sapp Railroad Contractor, is running for re-election on a platform of lower taxes, limited government, and support for rural infrastructure.   

As in Senate District 8, the legislative primaries were canceled because both Sapp and his Democratic challenger, Lee Butler, ran unopposed within their respective parties. They will compete directly in the November general election, where the district's strong Republican registration margin (R+48.2) places the incumbent in a highly favorable position.   
County Commission and Municipal Contests
----------------------------------------

Local county commission and municipal contests represent the most direct channel of influence for Putnam County residents on issues like road maintenance, animal control, utility rates, and zoning policies.   

### Board of County Commissioners: District 4

The Republican primary for County Commission District 4 is a closely watched local contest between long-serving incumbent Larry Harvey and challenger Joseph "Joey" Agner.   

* **Larry Harvey (REP):** First appointed by Governor Rick Scott in 2014, Harvey is running for a fourth term. As the current chair of the First Coast Workforce Development Consortium, Harvey’s campaign focuses on economic development, infrastructure improvements, and leveraging his state-level connections to bring resources to Putnam County. Harvey has also taken a strong stance on local environmental and economic issues, opposing state-led efforts to remove the Rodman Reservoir dam on the Ocklawaha River (HB 981), arguing that dam removal would dry up West Putnam lakes and harm local recreation-based economies.   

* **Joseph "Joey" Agner (REP):** Agner is running on a platform of fiscal accountability and reform. He has focused his campaign on a detailed critique of county spending, pointing out that while county property tax revenues increased by approximately 125% between 2016 and 2025 (rising from $31.8 million to $71.7 million), residents continue to face sub-standard dirt roads and slow emergency response times. Agner has also raised concerns about local animal control, noting that while the Animal Services budget grew from $421,000 to $1.3 million over the past decade, zero citations were issued between 2016 and 2025 due to administrative shifts, which he argues has compromised public safety.

### City of Palatka Municipal Elections

The nonpartisan municipal contests in Palatka, the county seat, reflect a community focused on economic recovery and downtown revitalization.   

* **Palatka Mayor-Commissioner:** Incumbent Mayor Robbi Correa, a downtown merchant and the first woman elected to the office, is seeking re-election. Her campaign highlights her work on a $1 million downtown revitalization initiative to widen sidewalks, repave roads, and improve street lighting to support local merchants. She faces a notable challenge from former Mayor and Pastor Karl N. Flagg, who has raised $23,300—the largest campaign fund in the municipal field. The mayoral race also includes candidates Tatyiana Henry Daniel and Constance Daniels. If no candidate receives a simple majority (50% plus one vote) in the August primary, the top two finishers will compete in a runoff during the November general election.   

* **Palatka City Commissioner (Group 2):** This race is a head-to-head contest between former Commissioner James Norwood, who has raised $12,871, and Jamaad C. Batts, Sr., who has raised $2,100.   

### Seventh Judicial Circuit Court: Group 26

The nonpartisan primary for Group 26 of the Seventh Judicial Circuit—which serves Flagler, Putnam, St. Johns, and Volusia counties—features candidates Carleen Leffler and Jeanne Stratis. Because this is a circuit-wide judicial election, all registered voters in Putnam County, regardless of party affiliation, are eligible to cast ballots in this race during the August 18 primary. Both candidates have deep roots in the regional legal system and are campaigning on their qualifications, judicial temperament, and commitment to constitutional adherence. Under Florida judicial election rules, if a candidate secures a majority of the vote in the primary, they are elected, and the race will not appear on the November general election ballot.   
Statewide Constitutional Amendments
-----------------------------------

Putnam County voters will decide three legislatively referred constitutional amendments on the November general election ballot. These measures require a 60% supermajority vote to pass and have direct implications for local government budgets, personal property taxes, and state financial reserves.

### Amendment 1: Changes to Budget Stabilization Fund Amendment

This joint resolution (HJR 5019) proposes to increase the cap on the state's Budget Stabilization Fund—commonly referred to as the "rainy day fund"—from 10% to 25% of the state's net general revenue collections.

The amendment’s fiscal model can be represented by the fund's maximum allowed balance:

BSFMax​=0.25×GRprior​

where GRprior​ represents the net revenue collections of the general revenue fund for the last completed fiscal year. If approved, the amendment would require the legislature to make an annual transfer of $750 million (or the amount needed to reach the 25% cap, whichever is less) into the fund. These transfers could be suspended in years with revenue shortfalls, emergency spending, or if the legislature declares a critical state need by a two-thirds vote.   

Proponents argue that maintaining larger reserves protects the state during economic recessions and natural disasters, which secures higher credit ratings and lowers borrowing costs. Opponents contend that locking up to 25% of general revenue reduces the funds available for annual appropriations in critical areas such as public school funding, healthcare, and infrastructure improvements.   

### Amendment 2: Exempt Tangible Personal Property Used for Agriculture or Agritourism from Property Taxes

This amendment (HJR 1215) would exempt tangible personal property—such as tractors, harvest equipment, and specialized farming tools—from ad valorem property taxes, provided the equipment is habitually located on agricultural land and owned by the landowner or leaseholder.   

This measure is designed to support Florida's agricultural and agritourism sectors by lowering operational overhead costs. In Putnam County, where agricultural production is a key component of the local economy, this tax exemption could provide financial relief to local farming operations. However, as with other tax exemptions, it would result in a marginal reduction in the county's tangible personal property tax base.   

### Amendment 3: Homestead Tax Exemptions, Property Assessments, and Spending Restrictions

Initiated during a special legislative session called by Governor Ron DeSantis, Amendment 3 represents a significant proposed change to Florida's property tax system.   

* **Tax Exemption Expansion:** The amendment would increase the current non-school homestead exemption from $25,000 to $150,000 in 2027, and to $250,000 in 2028. Beginning in 2029, this exemption would be indexed to inflation based on the Consumer Price Index. Crucially, this expansion would apply only to non-school property taxes; school district levies would remain subject to the existing tax structure.   

* **Assessment Cap Reduction:** For non-homestead properties (such as commercial real estate, rental units, and second homes), the amendment would reduce the maximum annual assessment increase cap from 10% to 5%, also excluding school district taxes.   

* **Spending Preemption:** The amendment would restrict local governments, limiting county and municipal spending of remaining property tax revenues to state-defined "core public needs," such as public safety, infrastructure, education, and natural resource conservation.   

* **New Resident Phase-In:** To protect the existing tax base, individuals who establish residency in Florida after December 31, 2026, would receive a smaller homestead exemption ($50,000) and would have to maintain residency for five years before qualifying for the full exemption.   

#### Causal Analysis and Ripple Effects

For a rural, low-income county like Putnam, where 22.4% of residents live below the poverty line, the economic consequences of Amendment 3 are complex. Supporters argue the measure provides direct tax relief to homeowners struggling with inflation and rising insurance costs. However, state economists estimate the amendment would reduce local government revenues statewide by nearly $12 billion annually by 2031.   

Because Putnam County has a lower median home value, a $250,000 homestead exemption could exempt a significant portion of its residential properties from non-school ad valorem taxation. This loss of revenue would impact local government services. To maintain essential services, county commissioners might have to implement alternative local fees (e.g., park fees, utility surcharges) or seek state grants, potentially shifting the tax burden to non-homestead properties, which could lead to higher commercial rents.   

### Putnam County's Ongoing School Construction Bond

In November 2022, Putnam County voters approved a $300 million school bond referendum to finance a comprehensive, 10-year facility revitalization plan. This bond is being repaid through a localized increase in property taxes over a 30-year period.   

Phase one of this initiative is currently underway, with construction on two new elementary schools—located in Palatka and Crescent City—scheduled for completion, alongside phased upgrades to Crescent City Junior-Senior High School. Phase two of the project will transition to upgrading Melrose Elementary School. This local capital initiative runs parallel to the proposed statewide amendments, highlighting the ongoing effort to balance infrastructure investment with property tax policy.

Analytical Conclusions
----------------------

The 2026 electoral landscape in Putnam County highlights the challenges of balancing tax relief with local infrastructure and service demands in a rural community.

Statewide ballot initiatives like Amendment 3 reflect a broader effort to reduce the property tax burden. However, the resulting revenue reductions are felt most acutely in economically constrained counties like Putnam, which have limited tax bases and high poverty rates. This dynamic places greater importance on the administrative decisions of local officials, such as the County Commission and Municipal Leaders.   

The competitive primary for County Commission District 4 illustrates this central challenge. Voters are presented with a choice between the experienced, state-aligned leadership of Larry Harvey, who focuses on securing regional grants, and the fiscal reform platform of Joey Agner, who calls for greater accountability and more efficient resource allocation 1 . Ultimately, the outcomes of these local and statewide contests will determine the county's fiscal policy and developmental direction for years to come.
